*On structural biology, artificial intelligence, and the continuity of scientific reasoning*

When I worked in computational structural biology, I spent a great deal of time examining *decoys*—candidate molecular structures generated by Rosetta. Some looked remarkably close to the structures I expected. Others had symmetry problems so severe that the resulting assemblies were physically outrageous.

They were hilarious to look at.

The computer had no particular obligation to produce the structure I envisioned. Rosetta explored possible conformations according to its algorithms, scoring functions, and the constraints I supplied. A convincing result might reflect a reasonable model of the molecular system. An absurd result might expose a problem with the computational setup. Neither conclusion followed automatically from appearances.

I had to investigate.

I examined the geometry, checked the relevant physical principles, made measurements, and compared the predictions against available data. I questioned my assumptions, examined how well the models fit the evidence, and returned to the governing constraints when the results failed to make sense.

Then I changed something and ran the calculation again.

Each iteration gave me another opportunity to test my reasoning. Sometimes the results improved. Sometimes they exposed problems I had not anticipated. Occasionally, a surprising prediction forced me to reconsider what I thought the structure should look like in the first place.

I learned not to confuse a plausible computational result with a correct one. The model proposed possibilities; I had to determine what the evidence justified.

Recently, while building something entirely different, I recognized that I was doing essentially the same thing.

I had been developing *The Institute*, a personal organization intended to coordinate my scientific, technical, and creative projects. I wanted a council of specialized AI agents that could examine problems from different perspectives, evaluate competing priorities, and provide recommendations.

Strategy would assess opportunities. Research would investigate evidence. Finance would examine resources and consequences. An independent quality-assurance function would challenge the reasoning. I would retain authority over decisions, while separate agents executed approved work.

The concept made sense in my head. Translating it into an architecture proved more interesting.

I asked ChatGPT to diagram the organization. It produced an elaborate arrangement of advisory functions, operational divisions, institutional memory, and execution layers. The result looked impressive.

Something was missing.

Accounting.

I pointed that out, and the model incorporated it. Unfortunately, the revised architecture became even more complicated. I asked it to simplify the system. It produced another diagram. I simplified the requirements again.

Eventually, I asked to see only myself and the council, with three examples of each participant's responsibilities.

That produced a cleaner organizational chart, but it still did not capture what I wanted.

I recognized the problem while looking at the diagram. The council did not feel like a static hierarchy. It felt like a process. Information needed to move between the advisors, the decision-maker, the execution functions, and the institutional memory that preserved what happened.

I told ChatGPT that the system seemed to flow more than the diagram suggested.

The next version represented a recurring cycle: I establish direction, the council deliberates, I evaluate its recommendations, agents execute approved work, and the results return as evidence for subsequent decisions.

There it was.

I had not supplied that complete diagram at the beginning. I discovered the appropriate structure through successive proposals, evaluations, and revisions.

And suddenly, the entire exercise felt familiar.

I had spent years doing exactly this with computational models.

The early council diagrams were analogous to Rosetta decoys. They represented candidate solutions generated under the instructions and assumptions I had supplied. Some captured important relationships. Others contained obvious structural problems. I could not accept them merely because they looked coherent.

I had to examine their internal logic, identify inadequate constraints, and revise the model.

The difference was that I could now describe the problem in ordinary language rather than construct specialized computational inputs. The range of possible applications had expanded enormously, but my method of investigating them had not fundamentally changed.

This recognition emerged from an entirely different question.

After building the council, I asked ChatGPT how much of the architecture came from my own ideation and how much came from AI. It proposed that roughly 70–80% originated with me and the remainder with the model.

I enjoyed the answer, but I could not defend those numbers.

What would such a calculation measure? The original objective? The number of revisions? The individual architectural components? The importance of a particular suggestion?

The AI had made meaningful contributions. It proposed organizational distinctions, generated alternative structures, and helped me recognize relationships I had not yet articulated. One particularly useful distinction separated advisory functions from execution functions. I adopted it because it clarified the system.

I would misrepresent the process if I described those contributions as mere formatting.

But I would also misrepresent the process if I treated the machine's ability to generate alternatives as equivalent to establishing which alternative deserved adoption.

I had encountered that distinction throughout my computational research.

Rosetta could explore structural possibilities far more efficiently than I could by hand. Its calculations sometimes supported my hypotheses and sometimes challenged them. I did not need to personally calculate every candidate structure to contribute intellectually to the research.

My work involved defining the scientific problem, determining which constraints mattered, examining competing predictions, and evaluating their agreement with physical principles and experimental evidence.

I see the same responsibilities in my present work with AI.

There are important differences between the systems. Rosetta uses specialized molecular representations, sampling algorithms, and approximate energy functions. Language models generate responses from learned statistical relationships and the context of their instructions. Their mechanisms are not equivalent, nor are the standards by which we should evaluate their outputs.

Structural biology also offers something that organizational design often lacks: quantitative measurements against which we can test predictions.

I cannot calculate the free energy of a council.

I can, however, examine whether its recommendations survive independent scrutiny, whether its reasoning contains unsupported assumptions, and whether its decisions produce useful results. I can record what the system predicted, what I approved, what actually happened, and where the outcomes contradicted our expectations.

I also need to remember that an AI council can reproduce the same error across several advisors. Multiple recommendations do not constitute independent evidence simply because different agents produced them. Giving one agent responsibility for quality assurance does not guarantee that it will recognize a shared mistake.

The architecture itself remains a hypothesis. Its value depends on what it accomplishes.

That brings me back to the insight I found most satisfying.

I initially thought I was learning an entirely new way of working with artificial intelligence. In one sense, I was. The technology gives me extraordinary leverage across problems that previously required specialized tools or substantial manual effort.

But the underlying intellectual discipline is something I already know.

I establish objectives. I formulate constraints. I generate candidate solutions and examine their behavior. I measure what I can, compare the results against evidence, and revise my assumptions when the model fails.

I also try to remember that my preferred result is not necessarily the correct one.

The machine can surprise me. It can introduce a useful distinction, reveal an inconsistency, or suggest an alternative I had overlooked. Those contributions make the collaboration valuable. They also give me more reasons to maintain the habits that computational science taught me.

I no longer need to perform every calculation or construct every intermediate model myself. I do need to understand what I am testing, why the results matter, and what would persuade me to change my mind.

That was true when I studied molecular structures. It remains true as I build an AI-assisted organization.

After all these years, I'm still examining decoys. They just have job titles now.
