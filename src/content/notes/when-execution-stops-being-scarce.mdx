For much of industrial history, technology primarily substituted for **physical labor**. Machines multiplied muscle. For people whose comparative advantage was abstraction—scientists, engineers, analysts, writers, programmers—the implicit bargain was almost the opposite: *the mind was the scarce resource.*

AI is beginning to attack that scarcity.

That produces a different psychological problem.

A person can now conceive a software system, have one model architect it, another implement pieces, another test it, and another administer the surrounding workflow. The bottleneck increasingly is not typing code, moving files, writing boilerplate, searching literature, or formatting information.

**The bottleneck is becoming the decision about what is worth doing.**

The old knowledge-worker problem was:

> I have fifty things I want to accomplish and only enough labor to do three.

The emerging problem is:

> I can initiate fifty things. Which three actually deserve a piece of my life?

The second problem can be more exhausting, not less.

AI reduces **execution cost**, but it dramatically expands the **possibility space**. Every idea becomes more plausible. Every curiosity can become a project. Every project can become a product. Every dataset can become an analysis. Every workflow can be automated. Every piece of knowledge can be reorganized, synthesized, published, monetized, or turned into software.

The mind therefore loses an old stopping signal:

*I simply do not have time to do that.*

This creates a form of **psychological labor exhaustion**. The burden moves upstream. Instead of exhaustion from performing every task, there is exhaustion from continually deciding which possible tasks, projects, and directions deserve existence at all.

## Intentional constraint

The increasingly valuable skill may not be greater productivity. AI can manufacture enormous amounts of productivity.

The scarce skill becomes **intentional constraint**.

I see at least four uses of human time becoming increasingly important:

1. **Direct human experience** — lifting, walking, photography, reading, playing, spending time with people, and experiencing the physical world. These activities do not need to produce anything.
2. **Judgment** — deciding what matters, what is true, what is worth building, what risks are acceptable, and what deserves attention.
3. **Creation and direction** — originating hypotheses, systems, aesthetics, questions, and goals while machines increasingly perform implementation.
4. **Maintenance of autonomy** — health, finances, relationships, skills, property, and the infrastructure that preserves independence.

Conspicuously absent is the requirement to be **maximally productive**.

If machines increasingly perform the instrumental work required to achieve an objective, then choosing objectives becomes a larger fraction of human life.

That is not necessarily utopia. It may be deeply uncomfortable, particularly for people accustomed to deriving satisfaction from competence. There was always another mountain: learn the technique, solve the problem, do the work.

Now it is increasingly possible to stand at the bottom of a mountain and say:

> Machine, build me a road.

That leaves a stranger question:

**Why was I climbing this mountain in the first place?**

I do not think the answer is to reject the machines or to fill the newly available capacity with still more work. The useful distinction is between **things I genuinely want to experience** and **things I merely want completed**.

Let AI devour the second category.

Protect the first.

And spend scarce human attention deciding the difference.
