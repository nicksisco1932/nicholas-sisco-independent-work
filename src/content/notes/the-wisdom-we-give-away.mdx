*On aging, self-knowledge, and becoming subject to one's own principles*

Lately I have had the strange feeling of becoming someone I have not fully recognized as myself.

Not in the sense of alienation. Almost the opposite.

Over time, certain judgments have become increasingly natural to me. I know what I would tell someone who has spent years proving what no longer needs to be proved. I know what I would say to someone who keeps measuring his life by titles, recognition, or what an institution thinks of him. I know that being capable of carrying more does not mean you are obligated to keep picking things up. And I know the importance of protecting time, health, relationships, and the parts of a life that cannot simply be rebuilt later.

I have said versions of these things to other people often enough that they no longer feel like borrowed ideas. They have become part of the way I interpret a life.

What I had not fully noticed was that I did not always extend those same judgments to myself.

The recognition came when I encountered those same judgments reflected back toward me. Removed from the immediate pressure of being the person inside the problem, they sounded at once familiar and strangely new. I knew the principles. I recognized the voice behind them. What I had not recognized as clearly was myself among the people to whom they applied.

That was the catalyst.

The interesting question was no longer where the advice had come from. It was how a person could articulate a principle clearly, apply it generously to others, and still fail to see that the same principle might apply to him.

Why can a person sometimes see a life more clearly from the outside than from within it?

And what happens when, after years of giving certain wisdom away, he finally encounters it as a claim upon his own life?

## The mirror

Advice is usually treated as information about the person receiving it.

It can also be evidence about the person giving it.

Listen to what someone consistently tells people he cares about. Leave the job that is consuming you. Keep your promise. Stop confusing someone's approval with your worth. Call your mother. Save your money. Go to the doctor. You do not have to prove this again. You owe her an apology.

None of those statements becomes true merely because it is sincerely offered. Advice can be sentimental, inherited, self-serving, simplistic, or simply wrong. A person may also understand someone else's circumstances poorly.

Still, repeated sincere advice tells us something. It reveals principles the adviser is prepared to endorse when he is not the immediate object of judgment.

That philosophy may be clearer in his treatment of others than in his treatment of himself.

This is the point at which a familiar complaint about hypocrisy becomes less interesting than a question about selfhood.

Why does the adviser exclude himself from his own moral jurisdiction?

By *moral jurisdiction* I do not mean that every piece of practical advice is a moral law. I mean something more basic: if I claim that a principle applies to human beings under certain conditions, and I occupy materially similar conditions, then I owe myself an explanation if I exempt myself.

"I am me" is not an explanation.

Suppose I tell a colleague that twenty years of excellent work have already established his competence. He does not need to treat every new demand as another trial of whether he belongs. I can see his life from a useful distance: the work already done, the opportunities foregone, the finite time being spent, the difference between responsibility and endless proving.

Then I return to my own work on Monday morning and behave as though every problem is an examination.

The contradiction may be obvious from the outside and nearly invisible from within.

That is not necessarily hypocrisy. It may not even be a failure of will. I may never have reached the point at which will becomes the issue.

I may possess the principle without recognizing my own case as an instance of it.

Psychology has a nearby name for part of this asymmetry. In 2014, Igor Grossmann and Ethan Kross studied what they called **Solomon's paradox**. Across three experiments involving 693 participants, people reasoned more wisely about another person's relationship conflict than about their own. Their measures of wiser reasoning included acknowledging uncertainty, considering other perspectives, recognizing the possibility of change, and searching for compromise. In two of the experiments, asking participants to adopt a distanced perspective on their own situation eliminated the observed self-other asymmetry. The effect was similar in younger and older adult groups.[1]

The experiments are narrower than the claim I am making here. They concern particular conflicts and particular measures of reasoning. They do not establish that advice to others is generally superior, or that psychological distance gives us privileged access to truth.

But they demonstrate something important: changing the position from which a person considers a problem can change the quality of the reasoning applied to it.

The information available to the mind is not the whole story. The location of the observer matters.

The problem is older than the psychology used to describe it.

Aristotle's treatment of practical wisdom and *akrasia* comes surprisingly close. The usual shorthand for *akrasia* is weakness of will: I know what I should do and nevertheless do something else. But Aristotle's actual treatment is more subtle. In Book VII of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, he distinguishes possessing knowledge from using it. He notes that a person can speak the language of knowledge without having fully assimilated it, much as a new learner can repeat propositions that have not yet "become part" of him. He also distinguishes universal judgment from recognition of the relevant particular case.[2]

Book VI makes the point from another direction. Practical wisdom is not concerned only with universals; it must recognize particulars, because action occurs in particular circumstances. Aristotle even connects practical wisdom with experience and time. A young person can master abstract subjects, he argues, while practical judgment requires familiarity with the kinds of particulars that experience supplies.[3]

So the distinction I am interested in should not be presented as a missing step Aristotle somehow overlooked. It sits inside a problem he was already examining: the difficult passage from what one can state in general to what one can see and do in a concrete life.

The mirror gives us one way through that problem.

Another person can make the principle easier to see.

And then, sometimes, the principle returns.

Seneca described something remarkably close to this in a letter to Lucilius. Anticipating the obvious objection—who are you to advise someone else when you still have faults of your own?—he refuses the posture of the perfected teacher. He describes himself and Lucilius as patients sharing a remedy. Then he says, in Richard Gummere's translation:

> "Listen to me, therefore, as you would if I were talking to myself."[4]

The line is nearly the whole problem in miniature.

The other person is not merely the recipient of wisdom. He can become the condition under which the adviser is finally able to hear himself.

## The changing reader

I read Aristotle often as an undergraduate in 2009, during a dark period of my life.

I am cautious about making too much of that fact. I do not remember some cinematic scene in which a particular sentence suddenly saved me, nor do I want to invent one after the fact. Memory is too cooperative with stories we decide we want to tell.

What matters is simpler.

I encountered Aristotle then.

I encounter him differently now.

The words did not spend seventeen years acquiring new meanings in the book. The reader changed.

At twenty-something, I could understand a proposition about practical wisdom, habit, flourishing, or acting against better judgment. I could follow an argument. I could explain it. But there were kinds of evidence I did not yet possess because they take time to accumulate.

I had not yet watched enough decisions finish unfolding.

I had not seen enough institutions from the inside, enough ambitions change shape, enough relationships deepen or disappear, enough apparently urgent problems become irrelevant, enough small choices become trajectories.

This is not an argument that age produces wisdom. It plainly does not. Experience can be repeated without being examined, and years can reinforce a bad model as easily as they can correct it. Grossmann and Kross found no simple advantage of older age in reasoning about one's own personal conflicts.[1]

Time supplies observations.

Integration is something else.

Psychologists who study **self-continuity** ask how connected people feel to their past and future selves. The literature suggests that this sense of continuity may change across adulthood, and that thinking of oneself as extended through time matters for behavior and identity.[5] Philosophy has approached the same territory from another direction. Paul Ricoeur's work on narrative identity resisted the idea that the self is simply a fixed object immediately transparent to itself. The title of his later work, *Oneself as Another*, captures something useful here even before one enters the details of his argument: self-understanding can require mediation, interpretation, and a perspective that is not identical to immediate first-person experience.[6]

Aging creates an unusual observer.

The forty-year-old is continuous with the twenty-year-old and yet capable of seeing him almost as another person.

I remember what earlier versions of myself felt from the inside. I can also judge them from a distance they could not possess.

That double position is one of the peculiar gifts—and dangers—of a long memory.

The danger is arrogance. The later self knows the outcome and can mistake hindsight for superior character. He can rewrite uncertainty as obviousness. He can condemn the younger person for failing to possess information that did not yet exist.

But the gift is having years of evidence.

A person can watch his own theories encounter reality.

Some survive. Some fail. Some turn out to have been adaptations to conditions that no longer exist. Some were useful precisely because they were temporary.

This makes aging less like accumulating a library and more like repeatedly revising a model while remaining inside the system being modeled.

The self doing the interpretation is also one of the things being interpreted.

That is why old ideas can become newly available.

There are propositions we can understand before we can fully use them. We can learn the vocabulary before life has supplied enough particulars to know where the concept belongs. Years later, an apparently unrelated event gives the old idea a referent.

The sentence has not become deeper.

Our contact with it has.

This also explains why another person's problem can suddenly illuminate our own history.

A younger colleague tells you he is afraid to leave a prestigious job because leaving would make the previous ten years feel wasted. You immediately see the sunk-cost error. You know that prior expenditure cannot be recovered by further expenditure. You tell him so.

Then, perhaps hours later, you notice another domain of your life in which you have been using almost exactly the opposite logic.

The other person's situation has stripped away enough of your own history, fear, pride, identity, and local urgency for the principle to become visible.

The mirror did not create the principle.

It changed the angle.

This is why the process can feel like discovering something you already knew.

Knowledge is not stored in the mind as a single clean object that is either present or absent. A person can possess a general rule, apply it competently in one part of life, fail to notice that another part has the same shape, and then abruptly see the connection.

The philosophical interest lies in that abrupt return.

A principle that has traveled outward comes back carrying information about its author.

## What the mirror owes us

There is an obvious danger in all of this.

If the mirror only ever tells us to rest, leave, protect ourselves, forgive ourselves, and stop trying so hard, then we have not developed a philosophy of self-knowledge. We have developed a sophisticated permission machine.

A serious mirror has to be capable of returning an accusation.

Imagine that I tell a friend: you gave your word; being irritated now does not erase the commitment you made when you were calm. If I later realize I have been rationalizing my way out of one of my own commitments, the reflected principle does not release me. It binds me more tightly.

Or imagine that I tell someone he owes another person an apology. I see immediately that intention is not the same thing as consequence. Then I recognize a situation in which I have been defending myself with exactly the distinction I refused to accept from him.

The mirror has done its work only if I permit the symmetry.

But symmetry is not enough either.

Circumstances can differ in morally relevant ways. A person with dependents may not be free to take the risk he recommends to a friend. A physician, commander, parent, or fiduciary may bear obligations that another person does not. A principle that sounds universal at conversational distance may dissolve when exposed to details.

The correct question is therefore not:

**Why am I failing to follow my own advice?**

It is:

**Does this principle survive scrutiny, and if it does, what relevant difference would justify applying it differently to me?**

Sometimes there is a good answer.

Sometimes there is not.

And sometimes the mirror reveals that the principle itself should be discarded.

A person can spend years telling others never to depend on anyone because dependence once made him vulnerable. The advice may be sincere. It may also be a scar masquerading as a philosophy.

Consistency would not make him wiser.

Revision might.

This is why I no longer like a simple picture in which experience becomes knowledge, knowledge becomes wisdom, and wisdom finally becomes action. Life contains feedback. Acting changes what we learn. Failure can expose a defective principle. Success can tempt us to overgeneralize one that worked only under specific conditions. Other people challenge our abstractions. Time changes the constraints.

Maturity, if the word is useful at all, cannot mean perfect consistency between what a person says and what he does.

A perfectly consistent fool remains a fool.

What matters is a harder kind of coherence: the willingness to examine the gap between principle and conduct without automatically assuming which side is wrong.

Sometimes conduct needs to change.

Sometimes the principle does.

Sometimes the situation was misclassified.

And sometimes we discover that the principle was sound, the case really was ours, and we had quietly granted ourselves an exemption we would never have granted someone we loved.

That last possibility is the one that caught me.

I encountered familiar judgments from a position just far enough outside myself that I could hear them differently.

Their value was not that they arrived with some superior authority. It was that I heard them from outside my usual point of view.

For a moment, the principles were no longer tangled in the immediate pressures of my own case. I could hear them first as judgments about a human life, and only afterward realize that the life under consideration was also mine.

Then I recognized the voice.

That recognition does not finish anything. Knowing that I advise others one way does not prove that the advice is correct, that my circumstances are identical, or that I will act on it once its relevance becomes clear.

It does something smaller.

It removes one place to hide.

The principle is now on the table, alongside the life of the person who keeps giving it away.

I think that is one of the more interesting things about growing older. We accumulate not only experiences, but judgments about experiences. We hand those judgments to friends, students, colleagues, spouses, strangers, sometimes to people much younger than we are. We begin to sound, almost without noticing, like the older people we once sought out—or resisted.

And every so often one of those judgments returns.

The man who has spent years telling other people how to live is forced to ask whether he considers himself one of them.

Perhaps wisdom is not finally becoming the person who knows what to say.

Perhaps it is becoming less willing to make oneself the unexplained exception to what one says—and more willing, when the principle fails, to change the principle.

We give wisdom away.

Sometimes it comes back asking whether we meant it.
