*On merit, privilege, and the authority money cannot confer*

At a certain level of wealth, a person can stop working without stopping earning.

The engineers keep designing. The technicians keep repairing. Employees continue producing, customers continue buying, and the machinery of commerce carries on. An owner's fortune may grow even when their personal contribution does not.

There is nothing inherently illegitimate about this arrangement. Building a successful enterprise requires judgment, risk, and often extraordinary effort.

But it raises an interesting question.

When wealth becomes increasingly independent of personal labor, why do we continue interpreting the growing fortune as evidence of growing individual brilliance?

The more closely I examine that question, the less interested I become in any particular billionaire. The more revealing problem is the story we tell about success—and what happens when that story becomes a theory of human worth.

## The invisible inheritance

We like stories about people who build something from nothing.

The struggling entrepreneur. The garage startup. The visionary who saw what everyone else missed. These stories reassure us that effort and reward remain connected. Someone worked harder, took greater risks, made better decisions, and received what their abilities deserved.

Sometimes, much of that story is true.

But almost nobody begins with nothing.

We enter the world within circumstances we did not choose. Our families possess some combination of wealth, education, professional relationships, and institutional familiarity. These resources shape which opportunities become available long before we make an independent economic decision.

An affluent childhood cannot be reduced to the amount of money a parent eventually invests in a business.

It may mean better schools, earlier exposure to technology, useful connections, and a household in which entrepreneurship seems ordinary rather than improbable.

Perhaps most importantly, it can mean that failure is survivable.

Consider two people with comparable intelligence, ambition, and technical ability. Both identify a promising opportunity. Both understand the risks.

One has family resources sufficient to absorb a failed venture without jeopardizing housing, healthcare, or future opportunities. The other has no such protection.

They face the same probability of business failure, but entirely different consequences.

One risks capital. The other may risk the material foundation of their life.

That difference changes what each person can rationally attempt. It affects how long they can persist, how often they can fail, and whether they can afford to try again.

Research on entrepreneurship identifies family background as an important influence on opportunity, operating through capital, occupational experience, and social networks.[1]

None of this means privilege guarantees success. Nor does it diminish the genuine achievements of people who begin with advantages.

It simply means that success cannot be understood without examining the conditions that made it possible.

If we begin measuring merit only after the race has started, we have excluded the starting positions from our explanation of the finish.

## When ownership becomes achievement

As a business grows, something else happens.

What began as an individual's effort becomes an increasingly collective enterprise.

Engineers solve technical problems. Researchers develop new knowledge. Managers coordinate operations. Skilled workers build and maintain systems. Specialists identify failures that might be invisible outside their disciplines.

The organization becomes capable of accomplishments no individual within it could achieve alone.

Yet public recognition often moves in the opposite direction.

Thousands contribute. One person receives the credit.

We recognize the absurdity of this arrangement in other settings. Nobody imagines that a hospital administrator personally performs every successful surgery, develops every treatment protocol, or maintains every piece of medical equipment.

We understand that institutional achievement arises from distributed expertise.

But when an organization belongs to a celebrated entrepreneur, we become remarkably comfortable attributing its technical accomplishments to a single individual.

Founders may deserve substantial recognition. Identifying an opportunity, recruiting capable people, securing resources, and sustaining an enterprise are consequential achievements.

But assembling an organization of excellent engineers is not equivalent to personally possessing all their engineering expertise.

Nor do financial returns measure individual intellectual contributions.

An entrepreneur who retains substantial ownership can accumulate enormous wealth as a company's valuation increases. A scientist whose discovery makes that growth possible may receive a salary, a bonus, or a modest equity award.

The difference in compensation can be immense without implying a comparable difference in ability or contribution.

Ownership determines who receives the residual financial benefits of an enterprise. It is not an instrument for measuring intelligence.

And once capital begins generating substantial returns of its own, the relationship between labor and wealth becomes even less direct.[2]

At sufficient scale, a person may become wealthier during a relatively unproductive year than an exceptionally productive employee becomes during an entire career.

Wealth and merit may be related.

They are not the same variable.

## The manufacture of authority

The next transformation is more troubling.

Once someone becomes sufficiently wealthy, society begins treating their financial success as evidence of something broader than business competence.

Their views are sought on education, medicine, politics, morality, human nature, and the future of civilization.

Their pronouncements become headlines. Their preferences influence institutions. Their opinions acquire an audience that specialists working within those fields may never reach.

Some of this attention is understandable. Successful entrepreneurs can develop valuable judgment about organizations, incentives, technology, and risk.

But expertise is not infinitely transferable.

A successful aerospace entrepreneur may understand manufacturing constraints and launch economics. That does not establish expertise in developmental psychology, epidemiology, moral philosophy, or public welfare.

Someone can make excellent decisions within one domain while holding poorly examined assumptions about another.

Yet we increasingly use extraordinary wealth as a substitute for evaluating extraordinary claims.

The mechanism is straightforward.

Wealth purchases attention. Attention creates familiarity. Familiarity can produce credibility. Eventually, credibility acquired in one domain becomes mistaken for authority in another.

The result is an influence that has escaped its original evidentiary boundaries.

A fortune becomes a credential.

And the question is no longer whether an argument is well supported, but whether the person making it is sufficiently successful.

## The problem with empathy

This brings me to the observation that prompted this inquiry.

In February 2025, Elon Musk described empathy as the "fundamental weakness of Western civilization."

His broader argument was more qualified. Musk acknowledged that empathy can be beneficial but suggested it can also be exploited, producing decisions harmful to society.[3]

That concern is worth examining.

Empathy is not an infallible guide to moral judgment.

We may respond more strongly to one identifiable person's suffering than to the suffering of thousands. We may favor people who resemble us. We may confuse emotional intensity with evidence that a proposed intervention will actually help.

Psychologists and philosophers have examined these limitations extensively. Paul Bloom, for example, has argued that emotional empathy can distort moral decisions and that reasoned compassion may provide a better foundation for ethical behavior.[4]

These are legitimate distinctions.

But acknowledging the limitations of empathy is not the same as establishing that concern for other people constitutes a civilizational weakness.

Consider medicine.

A patient's suffering does not establish which treatment is effective. Compassion cannot replace clinical evidence, diagnostic reasoning, or an understanding of adverse effects.

But the patient's suffering is among the reasons medicine exists.

We do not improve healthcare by becoming indifferent to patients. We improve it by combining concern for their well-being with methods capable of determining which interventions actually benefit them.

The same principle applies to public policy.

Empathy cannot tell us how to allocate limited resources or resolve competing obligations. It can, however, help us recognize that other people experience those decisions as more than abstractions.

Their losses are real. Their circumstances matter. Their experiences may contain information unavailable to the person making the decision.

Compassion does not eliminate the need for judgment.

It gives judgment something important to account for.

And here the relationship between wealth and authority becomes particularly uncomfortable.

The people with the greatest influence over consequential decisions are not necessarily those who will experience their effects.

A wealthy policymaker can advocate reductions in public services without personally depending on those services. An affluent executive can recommend sweeping labor-market changes without confronting the financial insecurity those changes might impose.

Their recommendations are not automatically wrong. Financial independence is not a moral defect, just as material hardship does not guarantee sound judgment.

But insulation from consequences can remove an important source of corrective feedback.

When someone no longer experiences vulnerabilities that others cannot escape, understanding those vulnerabilities requires deliberate effort.

Empathy, understood as an attempt to recognize another person's experience, becomes more important under those conditions, not less.

The danger is not that wealthy people are incapable of compassion.

It is that people insulated from certain consequences may underestimate how much they have left to learn from those who are not.

## What success cannot prove

The answer is not to reverse the hierarchy and declare that poverty establishes virtue while wealth establishes moral failure.

That would reproduce the same error.

A successful entrepreneur may be brilliant, generous, and conscientious. A person born into privilege may use those advantages constructively. Someone who overcame hardship may nevertheless develop narrow or self-serving judgments.

Character cannot be read directly from socioeconomic position.

But neither can it be inferred from financial outcomes.

A serious account of achievement must distinguish the opportunities people received, the decisions they made, the work they personally performed, the contributions of others, and the returns generated by ownership.

These are related features of success, but they are not interchangeable measures of merit.

Once we separate them, another question becomes possible.

What gives someone the authority to tell others how they ought to live?

Certainly not wealth alone.

Relevant expertise matters. Evidence matters. Intellectual humility matters. So does the willingness to revise a position when its consequences become apparent.

And when discussing human welfare, there is an additional responsibility: taking seriously the experiences of those who must live with the decisions being proposed.

A fortune can provide extraordinary freedom. It can finance innovation, organize talent, and make previously impossible ambitions achievable.

It can also free someone from the economic necessity of being correct.

A person with sufficient wealth can survive mistakes that would devastate others. Their fortune may even increase while they make them.

We ought to recognize that asymmetry when evaluating their judgment.

Instead, we often do the opposite.

We mistake wealth for wisdom, influence for competence, and financial success for proof of individual genius.

We forget the advantages that made certain risks possible. We overlook the people whose labor made extraordinary achievements real. We confuse ownership of an outcome with personal mastery of everything that produced it.

And eventually, we allow those most insulated from ordinary consequences to speak as though that insulation has given them a superior understanding of everyone else's lives.

Perhaps that is what troubles me most about the mythology of the self-made billionaire.

Not that successful people receive recognition. Many deserve it.

But that we gradually forget what their success actually establishes.

The problem is not that wealthy people have opinions.

It is what happens when the rest of us stop requiring those opinions to earn their authority.

**A fortune may free a person from having to work.**

**It does not free their ideas from having to withstand scrutiny.**

And it certainly does not oblige anyone else to mistake their freedom from consequence for a superior understanding of what those consequences mean.
