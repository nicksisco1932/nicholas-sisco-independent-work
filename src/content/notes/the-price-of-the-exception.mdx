*How a society learns to tolerate what it claims to reject*

Imagine reading the history of a republic whose political parties mean nothing to you. You know its president only through the public record: how he responds to limits, treats critics, explains defeat, rewards loyalty, and uses power. Without the familiar names, what would you notice?

Then look beyond the leader. Who resisted? Who excused? What became easier to tolerate after the first boundary was crossed?

The first questions concern one person's conduct. The others concern a political culture—and what it learns to permit.

## The bargain

The phrase I keep returning to is *Faustian bargain*: an immediate gain purchased at a cost that may not become apparent until much later.

Political choices are rarely pure. People choose among imperfect alternatives, weigh competing interests, and sometimes support useful policies advanced by people whose conduct they dislike. A good outcome does not stop being good because its advocate behaved badly. But neither does it settle what had to be accepted to obtain it.

A vote is not an itemized contract. We cannot authorize only the agreeable parts of a politician's known pattern of behavior. Power arrives as a package, including foreseeable risks.

This does not mean every voter endorses every act. Some dispute the evidence. Some fear the alternative more. Others accept an uneasy compromise. A ballot tells us what someone chose; it cannot disclose an entire moral life.

Yet explanation does not erase consequence. If honesty, accountability, and restraint are principles, they must do more than decorate a speech. A principle is a boundary that still matters when crossing it would be useful. Repeatedly waiving it for a desired result changes what the principle does, whatever its supporters continue to call it.

One exception may be a difficult judgment. A succession of exceptions can teach everyone where the limits really are.

## When a boundary stops holding

Constitutions are written down. The habits that sustain them are not always written anywhere.

A leader presses against a limit. An institution resists, but the consequence is small. The leader presses again. Allies learn that defending the breach costs less than opposing it. Critics grow weary of sounding the alarm. Conduct that once demanded an explanation becomes familiar enough to require none.

The boundary remains on paper. Its enforcement becomes less certain.

No coordinated conspiracy is required. The process can proceed through indifference, exhaustion, calculation, disbelief, or the conviction that another goal matters more. Those making each accommodation need not imagine themselves changing their country. The effects can accumulate anyway.

Research helps explain parts of this process. Studies of American voters have found that partisan and policy preferences can limit their willingness to penalize candidates who violate democratic norms. Experiments have also shown how political signals can affect people's willingness to express exclusionary attitudes publicly. Yet large majorities in both major parties have reported opposition to political violence and breaches of democratic norms. These findings do not describe a single national character. They expose a gap between what people affirm and what they are prepared to enforce.[1–3]

A society can retain the language of integrity while making its violation less costly.

## What history permits us to see

History offers one advantage denied to the present: we know what came afterward.

In the 1850s, the American Party—the Know-Nothings—rose through anti-immigrant and anti-Catholic politics. A surviving membership document from 1854 pledged support for native-born officeholders and exclusion of Catholics. The movement won elections, fractured, and declined. We do not have to infer its exclusions from the accusations of its opponents. We can read them in its own commitments.[4]

Half a century later, a very different movement broke from an established party. Theodore Roosevelt's Progressive Party, remembered as the Bull Moose Party, advocated political reform, stronger corporate regulation, and social protections. It won more than a quarter of the presidential popular vote in 1912 but did not endure as a national electoral force.[5,7]

Its ambitions were not those of the Know-Nothings. Yet its history, too, allows us to compare aspiration with practice. The Bull Moose platform endorsed women's suffrage, while Black delegates from the South were excluded from its convention.[6] A reform movement could articulate an expansive principle and still make a consequential exception to it.

These parties do not form a continuous moral genealogy. The early Republican Party emerged chiefly in opposition to the expansion of slavery, and political coalitions subsequently changed. Party names cannot carry a fixed moral character across generations. What persists is a possibility within politics: that a narrower interest can displace a broader principle, sometimes while borrowing the principle's own language.

For those movements, we have platforms, electoral records, and outcomes. For our own moment, the archive is still being assembled. We can examine what has happened without claiming to know which events future historians will regard as decisive.

That uncertainty does not excuse documented conduct. It limits the certainty of our final story about it.

## What the bargain costs

The cost of an exception does not remain in the voting booth. Officials learn what carries consequences. Institutions learn whether resistance will be sustained. Citizens learn whether familiar words—honesty, fairness, equality before the law—still imply familiar obligations.

Independent assessments have recorded deterioration in measures of American democratic performance, particularly in 2025. Their methods and limitations matter; no index can supply a finished history of a country still changing.[8]

The loss I find hardest to measure, though, is ordinary trust.

Two neighbors can greet each other across a fence and speak warmly of integrity. Each may mean it. Neither knows what the other would excuse when honesty becomes inconvenient to something deeply valued. A political label cannot settle that question. It is no substitute for knowing a person.

Still, uncertainty has consequences. When the standards governing public life appear to change with the beneficiary, the words themselves begin to feel less dependable. We become unsure not only whether we agree, but whether we mean the same thing when we say we believe in fairness.

The answer cannot be to turn whole categories of strangers into moral verdicts. That would repeat, in private life, the very habit of reducing people to allegiance. But refusing that shortcut does not make repeated public choices morally weightless.

We can judge conduct without claiming possession of another person's inner life.

## What remains of a principle

The distant-republic experiment was never a disguise. It was an attempt to interrupt the reflex that tells us whom to defend before we have examined what happened.

The test must work in every direction. If I excuse in an ally what I condemn in an opponent, allegiance has displaced principle. The important question is not whether we can still recite our ideals, but whether they can still constrain us when honoring them costs something.

That is the price of the exception. Toleration can become precedent; precedent can become expectation; expectation can become culture. No one needs to announce the change. It can happen one useful compromise at a time.

**A republic does not have to vote to abandon its principles. It can make enough exceptions that those principles no longer constrain the people entrusted to uphold them.**
