## Level 1 · Foundations

Working muscle bears force. Cells respond to how that force is transmitted through their structures, not simply to the number printed on a weight.

## Level 2 · Mechanism

Force transmission through the cytoskeleton and membrane-associated structures can change biochemical regulation. Lipid signaling is one experimentally investigated route to mTOR-associated responses. [Hornberger TA 2006](#ref-hornberger2006)

<details class="advanced"><summary>Level 3 · Advanced</summary><p>Local strain, force duration, and structural geometry differ from external load. Integrins, cytoskeletal assemblies, and membrane lipids form candidate interacting routes; a single universal tension sensor is not established. <span class="citations"><a href="#ref-hornberger2006">Hornberger TA 2006</a></span></p></details>

## Inputs

- Motor-unit recruitment
- External resistance
- Muscle length and contraction geometry

## Outputs

- Mechanotransduction
- Remodeling signals

## Necessary versus sufficient

- **Contributory**: A training stimulus for adaptation; external load alone does not specify cellular force or eventual growth.

## Human evidence

<div class="evidence"><div class="badges"><span>Human intervention</span><span>Tracer study</span></div><p class="small">Model: Human · Directness: Indirect</p><p>Resistance exercise changes synthesis; these experiments do not isolate a unique molecular force sensor. <span class="citations"><a href="#ref-drummond2009">Drummond MJ 2009</a></span></p></div>

## Model-system evidence

<div class="evidence"><div class="badges"><span>Ex vivo</span></div><p class="small">Model: Animal · Directness: Direct</p><p>Isolated muscle experiments implicate phosphatidic-acid-associated signaling under mechanical stimulation. <span class="citations"><a href="#ref-hornberger2006">Hornberger TA 2006</a></span></p></div>

## What remains unresolved

Which force-sensing routes dominate during ordinary human training remains unresolved.

<aside class="callout"><h2>A common oversimplification</h2><p class="misconception">"Heavier weight always means more growth."</p><p><strong>Correction:</strong> External load is only one determinant of muscle loading; adaptation also depends on exposure and recovery.</p></aside>

## Practical relevance

Interpret loading in the context of the task and the muscle involved.
