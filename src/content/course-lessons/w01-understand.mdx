## Your starting point is information

Training is a repeated demand placed on a living system. A response during one session—breathing harder, fatigue or muscle signaling—is different from an adaptation that develops across sessions. The useful question is not whether a workout felt dramatic, but whether your capacity changes over time.

## Measure what your question needs

Choose one ability you want to develop: carrying groceries, completing a walk comfortably or learning a squat. Then identify something you can observe consistently. Record the task, its conditions and how it felt. A scale, watch or photograph cannot answer every question about health or adaptation.

## Keep the explanation provisional

A difficult session after a short night could reflect sleep, unfamiliar technique, insufficient food, illness or ordinary variation. A single observation cannot isolate the cause. Baseline notes help you choose a sensible experiment; they do not diagnose hormones, metabolic damage or fitness.

<aside class="callout"><h2>Worked example</h2><p>Maya wants stairs to feel easier. She notes the same flight, whether she carried a bag, and how long she paused at the top. Comparing that task over several weeks is more useful than interpreting one unusually high watch reading.</p></aside>

## Try it

- Write one ability-based goal and one observable sign of progress.
- List two conditions that could change the observation even if your fitness stayed the same.

Scenario alternative: Use Maya’s stair example and choose the conditions you would keep similar.
