## Understand — Adaptation starts with a question

### Your starting point is information

Training is a repeated demand placed on a living system. A response during one session—breathing harder, fatigue or muscle signaling—is different from an adaptation that develops across sessions. The useful question is not whether a workout felt dramatic, but whether your capacity changes over time.

### Measure what your question needs

Choose one ability you want to develop: carrying groceries, completing a walk comfortably or learning a squat. Then identify something you can observe consistently. Record the task, its conditions and how it felt. A scale, watch or photograph cannot answer every question about health or adaptation.

### Keep the explanation provisional

A difficult session after a short night could reflect sleep, unfamiliar technique, insufficient food, illness or ordinary variation. A single observation cannot isolate the cause. Baseline notes help you choose a sensible experiment; they do not diagnose hormones, metabolic damage or fitness.

> Worked example: Maya wants stairs to feel easier. She notes the same flight, whether she carried a bag, and how long she paused at the top. Comparing that task over several weeks is more useful than interpreting one unusually high watch reading.

Try it:

- Write one ability-based goal and one observable sign of progress.
- List two conditions that could change the observation even if your fitness stayed the same.

Scenario alternative: use Maya’s stair example and choose the conditions you would keep similar.

## Apply — Build a three-day baseline

For three ordinary days, note one movement opportunity, approximate sleep duration and a brief description of meals. Add a constraint such as a long commute or lack of cooking equipment. No body weight, calorie total or wearable is required. Leave uncertain values as estimates.

Describe movement by activity and duration rather than a watch’s calorie estimate. For sleep, distinguish time in bed from estimated time asleep. For food, write what was available and satisfying instead of labeling the day good or bad.

Practice:

- Use the baseline sheet for three days, or reconstruct three typical days from memory.
- Circle one repeated constraint and write one small change that addresses it.

Scenario alternative: complete the sheet for a fictional commuter with a limited food budget.

## Review — Choose your first experiment

A useful experiment specifies what, when and how much. “Walk for ten minutes after lunch on Tuesday and Thursday” is easier to evaluate than “be more disciplined.” Choose an action you can change if it does not fit.

You can schedule a session; you cannot guarantee a particular strength gain by Friday. Record whether the plan happened and what interfered. At the end of the week, keep, simplify or replace the experiment. If it worked, repeat it before making it harder.

Practice:

- Write a primary plan, a smaller fallback and a review date.
- Explain which observation would lead you to keep the plan and which would lead you to change it.

Scenario alternative: design a plan for Maya using only the scenario information.

## Evidence boundary

This week separates observations from explanations. Its baseline is a planning aid, not a diagnostic instrument. The production version cites CDC adult activity guidance and the AASM/SRS sleep-duration consensus.
